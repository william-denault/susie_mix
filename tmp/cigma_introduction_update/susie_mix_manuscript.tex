\begin{abstract} \bfseries \boldmath
% Start with one or two sentences of background
 

 Statistical fine-mapping has transformed the analysis of genetic associations by jointly analyzing thousands of correlated variants and prioritizing a small set of likely causal variants. These approaches are now routinely applied in genome-wide association studies and quantitative trait locus studies. Most fine-mapping analyses use additive genotype encoding, thereby assuming that phenotypes are determined by combinations of allele-dosage effects. Here, we show that this assumption can produce severely misleading inference when multiple causal variants act through different genotype-phenotype relationships. Through simulations and fine-mapping of gene expression in GTEx and [SECOND DATASET], we show that considering additive, dominant, and recessive genotype encodings can substantially alter the inferred signals and improve empirical credible-set coverage under non-additive simulation architectures. These findings establish genotype encoding as an important source of model misspecification in statistical fine-mapping.

\end{abstract}
\section{Introduction}
One of the central goals of modern human genetics is to understand how
single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) influence molecular phenotypes,
such as gene expression, and thereby contribute to complex traits and
disease. Expression quantitative trait locus (eQTL) studies, conducted
at large scale by the GTEx Consortium \cite{gtex_consortium_gtex_2020} and collected
across numerous studies by the eQTL Catalogue \cite{kerimov_compendium_2021}, have
identified thousands of genetic variants associated with transcript
abundance across tissues and cell types. These resources have become
central to translating GWAS associations into hypotheses about the
molecular mechanisms underlying complex traits.


Recent work emphasizes the importance of cellular context in this
translation. Chen et al. developed cell-type-informed genetic
mixed-model analysis (CIGMA) to partition gene-expression variation
into contributions from cell-type-shared and cell-type-specific
genetic effects while accounting for cell-to-cell variability
\cite{chen_cell_type_specific_2026}. Using population-scale single-cell
RNA sequencing, they found that genes with cell-type-specific genetic
regulation were enriched for autoimmune-trait heritability and showed
relationships with evolutionary constraint and enhancer complexity,
with replication of broad patterns in a second cohort. Their findings
support the view that bulk-tissue eQTL discovery can underrepresent
regulation relevant to complex traits.



Fine-mapping is the process of narrowing a genetic association signal
to a smaller set of potentially causal variants
\cite{schaid_genome-wide_2018}. This is challenging because nearby
variants are often correlated through linkage disequilibrium and can
therefore display similar association signals even when only one or a
few variants directly influence the phenotype. By analyzing correlated
variants jointly, fine-mapping methods aim to distinguish causal
variants from those that merely tag the same association signal.  Fine mapping procedure are sparse regression method that  regress the SNPs within a loci on a phenotype of interest (here the expression of a given gene) and outputs credible sets (CS) \cite{maller_bayesian_2012}, which are subsets of the SNPs that have a high probability of containing at least one causal variant. In presence of association CSs tend to be rather small, often  containing between 1 to a few dozen of SNPs. One of the key insinght of fina mappin method is that the number of CSs return by fine-mappping method provide an estimate on the number of  causal variants within a loci which  not possible to asses by simply scrutinizing marginal assocation as illustrated by \cite{wang_simple_2020}.

Therefore CS are now have become essential quantity for assessing genetic overlap between QTL study and molecular mechanisms underlying complex traits  So it is critical that CS are well calibrated to allow translational insight from QTL study to high level complex trait.

Credible sets are widely used to prioritize variants for functional
experiments and to assess whether molecular QTLs and complex-trait
associations may share an underlying causal variant. Their calibration
is therefore critical. Across repeated datasets, a well-calibrated
95\% credible set should contain the corresponding causal variant
approximately 95\% of the time. Poorly calibrated sets can create
unwarranted confidence in noncausal variants and propagate incorrect
conclusions into colocalization, causal-gene prioritization, and
functional follow-up analyses.







Genotype encoding is a complementary modeling choice. Cell-type
specificity concerns how an effect changes across cellular contexts;
genotype encoding concerns the relationship between allele dosage
and phenotype within a context. A variant can therefore have different
effect sizes across cell types while remaining additive within each.
CIGMA models this context dependence through shared and cell-type-specific
coefficients multiplying genotype dosage and estimates aggregate genetic
variance, rather than assigning posterior probabilities to individual
causal SNPs \cite{chen_cell_type_specific_2026}. It does not address
whether restricting genotype encoding to additive effects distorts
variant-level fine-mapping inference.

Under additive encoding, the modeled effect scales linearly with
allele count. Dominant encoding allows one copy to confer the same
effect as two, whereas recessive encoding requires two copies.
Dominant and recessive effects have been investigated in molecular
and complex traits \cite{cui_dominance_2023,palmer_analysis_2023}.
When such effects generate a phenotype, a fine-mapping model using
only additive predictors may be misspecified, with consequences for
both causal-SNP detection and credible-set calibration.

This misspecification need not result only in reduced power. If a
causal variant acts nonadditively, the additive encoding of a correlated
noncausal variant may approximate the observed phenotype more closely
than the additive encoding of the causal variant itself. The problem
can become particularly acute when multiple causal variants within a
locus act through different genotype encodings, a setting that we refer
to as \textit{heterogeneous modes of action}. Their combined signal may
then be better approximated by an additively encoded noncausal variant,
causing posterior support to move away from the true causal variants
and producing credible sets with substantially below-nominal coverage.

While some previous work have s have incorporated nonadditive effects into genetic
variable selection \cite{cheng_uncertainty_2022,peltola_bayesian_2012,sabourin_fine-mapping_2015}.
These include Bayesian model
averaging over additive, dominant, and recessive effects \cite{peltola_bayesian_2012}, group-penalized multi-SNP models of
additive and dominance effects \cite{sabourin_fine-mapping_2015},nonlinear extensions of the sum of single effects framework
\cite{cheng_uncertainty_2022}. 
It remains standard to use only additive coding in practive, the rare exception we found is and dominance-specific fine-mapping
using SuSiE \cite{palmer_analysis_2023}. 


The practical consequences for credible-set calibration when multiple
causal variants act through different genotype encodings remain an
important question, as does the extent to which these modeling choices
change fine-mapping conclusions in human eQTL data. Here, we compare
additive SuSiE with a mixed-coding analysis that includes additive,
dominant, and recessive predictors. We use simulations based on observed
GTEx genotypes to evaluate causal-SNP recovery, credible-set coverage,
and PIP-based discrimination, and examine how genotype encoding changes
fine-mapping results in GTEx gene-expression data.

This study complements the evidence from CIGMA that cellular context
matters for interpreting genetic regulation. We focus on the form of
the genotype--phenotype relationship and its consequences for localizing
causal variants. The simulations show that allowing alternative
encodings can improve recovery and coverage under non-additive
architectures, while retaining power trade-offs and undercoverage in
some conditions (Figs.~\ref{fig:sim-coverage-pure} and
\ref{fig:sim-coverage-mixed}). Differences between the GTEx fits motivate
further biological investigation; they do not alone establish which
variant or regulatory mechanism is causal.

\section{Results}

\paragraph{Simulation benchmark using observed genotype structure}

We compared additive SuSiE with unweighted SuSiE-mix using simulated
quantitative phenotypes and observed GTEx genotypes. The benchmark used
500 donors per dataset, one to five distinct causal SNPs, and target
proportions of variance explained (PVE) of 10\%, 20\%, 30\%, and 40\%.
We enumerated all 55 allocations of these causal SNPs to additive,
dominant, and recessive effects, giving 220 allocation--PVE conditions
with 400 scheduled replications each. The completed archive contained
87,988 usable datasets and 175,976 converged fits; 12 scheduled datasets
had zero genetic variance and were excluded without replacement.
Both methods were fitted to the same phenotype with an upper bound of
ten single-effect components. Evaluation used biological SNPs, combining
the retained encodings of each SNP in the mixed-model output (Methods).

The seven architecture classes comprise the three single-coding models,
the three possible pairs of codings, and all three codings together.
Within each class, results at a given causal count pool every compatible
allocation. Coverage figures retain the causal count on the horizontal
axis; pooled PIP curves combine all causal counts within each
architecture and PVE. The same 400 replication seeds were reused across
conditions, so the condition-specific results are dependent. The
figures report pooled point estimates across these shared replication
blocks.

\paragraph{Non-additive effects reduce coverage and causal-SNP recovery}

Allowing alternative genotype encodings improved credible-set coverage
under non-additive generating models, with the largest gains under
recessive effects (Figs.~\ref{fig:sim-coverage-pure} and
\ref{fig:sim-coverage-mixed}). For example, with three recessive causal
SNPs and PVE of 20\%, additive SuSiE achieved 83.0\% empirical coverage,
whereas SuSiE-mix achieved 96.5\%. The fraction of causal SNPs recovered
in the union of reported credible sets increased from 21.2\% to 79.2\%
(Fig.~\ref{fig:sim-power-pure}). At the same causal count and PVE under
dominant effects, coverage increased from 92.2\% to 95.4\%, and
credible-set recovery increased from 69.5\% to 78.8\%.

Gains also occurred when different causal SNPs followed different
encodings. With five causal SNPs spanning all three encodings and PVE
of 40\%, coverage increased from 87.3\% to 93.7\%, and credible-set
recovery increased from 61.1\% to 85.9\%
(Figs.~\ref{fig:sim-coverage-mixed} and \ref{fig:sim-power-mixed}).
These results show that the effect of additive misspecification extends
beyond loss of sensitivity: reported credible sets can also omit every
generating causal SNP more often than the nominal 5\% rate.

\paragraph{PIP-based detection at comparable empirical false discovery proportions}

PIP-based comparisons supported improved discrimination under
non-additive effects (Figs.~\ref{fig:sim-fdr-pure} and
\ref{fig:sim-fdr-mixed}). Pooling all causal counts at PVE of 40\%,
recessive-model detection power near 10\% empirical FDR increased from
6.1\% with additive SuSiE to 31.2\% with SuSiE-mix. Corresponding power
increased from 22.0\% to 30.5\% under dominant effects and from 11.5\%
to 24.8\% for architectures containing all three effect types.
Under additive-only effects, the corresponding powers were similar,
26.9\% and 26.6\%. These descriptive operating points use the last
sampled PIP threshold before the first crossing of 10\% empirical FDR
as the threshold decreases (Methods).

The ROC curves likewise showed the clearest separation under recessive
effects, while the additive-only curves were close
(Figs.~\ref{fig:sim-roc-pure} and \ref{fig:sim-roc-mixed}).
ROC curves stratified by causal count are provided in
Figs.~\ref{fig:sim-roc-pure-L1}--\ref{fig:sim-roc-mixed-L5}.
The absolute power at low empirical FDR remained limited, even where
ROC power was high: the false positive rate divides false selections
by all noncausal SNPs, whereas empirical FDR divides them by selected
SNPs. The power--FDR comparison therefore addresses a different and
more selective operating regime. Here empirical FDR is the pooled
false discovery proportion across simulated datasets; these curves
do not establish formal FDR control or PIP calibration.

\paragraph{Additive-case costs and remaining undercoverage}

The expanded model did not improve every performance measure. Under
additive-only effects with three causal SNPs and PVE of 20\%,
credible-set recovery decreased from 77.6\% to 74.7\%, despite similar
coverage (93.1\% versus 93.5\%). Across all additive-only conditions,
pooled credible-set recovery was 65.7\% for SuSiE and 63.3\% for
SuSiE-mix. Furthermore, SuSiE-mix did not uniformly attain nominal
coverage. With five causal SNPs spanning all three encodings and PVE
of 10\%, coverage improved from 81.9\% to 87.6\% but remained below
95\%, and credible-set recovery was only 7.5\% and 8.7\%, respectively.

Purity was also architecture dependent. For the three-recessive-SNP,
20\%-PVE example, mean minimum absolute within-set correlation increased
from 0.820 to 0.911; under the corresponding additive-only scenario it
decreased from 0.893 to 0.861 (Figs.~\ref{fig:sim-purity-pure} and
\ref{fig:sim-purity-mixed}). Mixed-model purity is computed on fitted
coding-specific predictors, so it is not a common additive-LD measure
of resolution. These results support a benefit from modeling
non-additivity while showing that broader predictor models retain
power and calibration trade-offs.


\paragraph{Descriptive comparison of fine-mapping results.}
After restricting the analysis to gene--tissue pairs with a marginal
association $P$ value below $10^{-8}$ and a mean read count of at
least 100, we retained [N] gene--tissue pairs representing [G] genes across
[T] tissues. The additive and mixed-coding SuSiE models inferred the same
number of credible sets in [X]\% of analyses. Among the [N$_1$] gene--tissue

[N$_D$] dominant credible-set signals. In contrast, only [P$_A$] and

\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{Implications and limits of the simulation evidence}

These simulations show that an additive restriction can impair both
causal-SNP recovery and credible-set coverage when causal effects are
non-additive. The improvement is most pronounced for recessive effects,
and remains apparent when comparison is based on SNP selection at
similar empirical false discovery proportions. This supports genotype
encoding as a consequential modeling choice, rather than treating a
change in the number of credible sets as sufficient evidence of improved
inference. At the same time, the expanded model incurs a modest
credible-set recovery cost in additive settings and does not uniformly
restore nominal coverage in weak, multicausal settings.

The benchmark evaluates a defined family of alternatives: Gaussian
phenotypes, a fixed sample size, at most five causal SNPs, and effects
drawn from the same additive, dominant, and recessive encodings offered
to SuSiE-mix. It therefore demonstrates the consequences of omitting
these encodings and the benefit of including the correct family, rather
than robustness to arbitrary nonlinear effects. Recessive effects were
selected only when at least five minor-allele homozygotes were available;
performance for rarer recessive effects is not established. Effect-size
heterogeneity, interactions, untyped causal variants, and additional
sample sizes also remain outside the present design.

Several distinctions matter for interpretation. Increased credible-set
recovery can reflect larger sets, so comparative set sizes are needed
before attributing every gain to improved resolution. Purity computed
under different codings does not resolve this issue. The point-estimate
comparisons also require uncertainty assessment that preserves shared
seeds across methods and conditions. ROC and power--FDR curves assess
discrimination; they do not establish that the numerical PIPs equal
empirical causal probabilities. Finally, the simulated outcome was not
subjected to the GTEx expression transformations or covariate adjustment,
and its fitting controls differed from the GTEx scan. The simulations
make the real-data disagreements worth investigating, but do not by
themselves validate an additional eQTL signal, a changed colocalization,
or a particular regulatory mechanism.

\subsection{GTEx data and analysis units}
We analyzed individual-level whole-genome sequencing genotypes and gene-level
RNA-sequencing read counts from GTEx v8 \cite{gtex_consortium_gtex_2020}.
The genotype input was the PLINK dataset
\texttt{GTEx\_Analysis\_2017-06-05\_v8\_WholeGenomeSeq\_866Indiv};
expression was obtained from
\texttt{GTEx\_Analysis\_2017-06-05\_v8\_RNASeQCv1.1.9\_gene\_reads.gct.gz}.
Subject phenotypes and sample attributes were obtained from the corresponding
GTEx v8 annotation files. Gene coordinates and protein-coding annotations
were taken from the GRCh38 release-103 gene annotation file
\texttt{Homo\_sapiens.GRCh38.103.chr.reformatted.collapse\_only.gene.gtf.gz}.
The candidate gene list comprised the intersection of protein-coding gene
symbols in this annotation and gene symbols in the expression matrix.
Gene-level annotations were retained regardless of their annotation source.
For a gene symbol with multiple expression entries or annotation records,
the first matching entry in each input was used.

RNA-sequencing samples were selected using the sample-attribute value
\texttt{SMAFRZE = RNASEQ}. Sample attributes were joined to subject phenotypes
using donor identifiers, and expression was matched using sample identifiers.
Analyses were performed separately for each gene and broad tissue category
defined by \texttt{SMTS}. Detailed tissue subtypes (\texttt{SMTSD}) were not
used to define separate analyses. Within a broad tissue category, each donor
contributed the first matching sample in the merged input order when multiple
samples were available. Donors were then matched to genotypes by subject
identifier. Samples with non-finite gene counts, non-finite or nonpositive
total read counts, or missing sex were excluded. A gene--tissue analysis
required at least 50 matched donors after these exclusions. The final numbers
of analyzed genes, tissue categories, donors, and gene--tissue pairs will be
reported as [TO INSERT].

\subsection{Cis-genotype extraction and quality control}
For each gene, the transcription start site (TSS) was defined as the annotated
start coordinate on the positive strand and the annotated end coordinate on
the negative strand. PLINK2 was used to extract SNPs within 500 kb on either
side of the TSS, with the lower coordinate bounded at zero. Extraction used
\texttt{--snps-only --max-alleles 2 --rm-dup exclude-all --maf 0 --recode A}.
Thus, the extracted predictors were biallelic SNP hard calls encoded as
0, 1, or 2; duplicate variant identifiers were excluded. Across all donors
in the genotype input, SNPs with any missing call or zero genotype variance
were removed.

Allele orientation and the remaining variant-level quality control were
determined before tissue-specific donor selection. If the frequency of the
coded allele exceeded one half, genotypes were replaced by $2-G$, so that
the coded allele was the less frequent allele in the full genotype sample.
Only variants with minor-allele frequency strictly greater than 0.05 were
retained. Hardy--Weinberg equilibrium was assessed using the Pearson
chi-square statistic with one degree of freedom, comparing the observed
genotype counts with $n(1-f)^2$, $2nf(1-f)$, and $nf^2$, where $f$ is the
coded-allele frequency and $n$ is the number of genotype donors. Variants
with $P<10^{-8}$ were excluded. The full-sample allele orientation was
retained in every tissue, and no additional tissue-specific
Hardy--Weinberg test, minor-allele-frequency threshold, or
linkage-disequilibrium pruning was applied.

\subsection{Expression preprocessing}
For a retained donor $i$, let $c_i$ denote the raw count for the target gene
and let $T_i$ denote the sum of counts over all genes in that donor's selected
expression sample. Within each gene--tissue analysis, the library-size factor
was $s_i=T_i/\overline{T}$, where $\overline{T}$ is the mean total count over
the retained donors. We calculated $z_i=\log(1+c_i/s_i)$ and transformed
these values to normal quantiles. For a vector $v$ of length $n$, the
transformation was
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal{R}(v_i)=\Phi^{-1}\left\{\frac{\mathrm{rank}(v_i)-1/2}{n}\right\},
\end{equation}
where $\Phi$ is the standard normal cumulative distribution function and
tied values receive their average rank. We fitted an ordinary least-squares
regression of $\mathcal{R}(z_i)$ on an intercept and donor sex, coded as the
GTEx \texttt{SEX} variable minus one. The residuals from this regression
were subjected to the same normal-quantile transformation to obtain the
phenotype $y_i$ used in both fine-mapping models.

Sex was the only adjustment covariate in this implementation. Age, detailed
tissue subtype, sequencing batch, genotype principal components, and latent
expression factors were not included. Genotype predictors were not
residualized on sex. Analyses with non-finite final phenotype variance or
variance at most $10^{-12}$ were skipped. Mean and median read counts used
in downstream summaries were calculated from the original gene counts
among the retained donors, before expression transformation.

\subsection{Additive and mixed-coding fine-mapping}
For each retained biological SNP $j$, we constructed additive, dominant,
and recessive predictors:
\begin{equation}
 X_{ij,A}=G_{ij},\qquad
 X_{ij,D}=\mathbf{1}\{G_{ij}\geq 1\},\qquad
 X_{ij,R}=\mathbf{1}\{G_{ij}=2\}.
\end{equation}
The additive analysis used only $X_A$. The mixed-coding analysis used the
concatenated matrix $(X_A,X_R,X_D)$, with each column representing a
SNP--coding pair. Within the matched tissue sample, columns were retained
only if they had positive standard deviation and a column sum of at least
five. For additive coding this is a requirement on the number of coded
allele copies; for dominant coding it requires at least five carriers;
for recessive coding it requires at least five coded-allele homozygotes.
The positive-variance requirement also excludes binary predictors equal
to one in every donor. These filters were applied separately to each
encoding, allowing a SNP to retain some encodings while losing others.
Analyses with no remaining additive or mixed predictors were skipped.

Both models were fitted using the individual-level \texttt{susie} function
in \texttt{susieR} \cite{wang_simple_2020}, with $L=10$ single-effect
components, \texttt{standardize = FALSE},
\texttt{estimate\_prior\_method = "EM"}, and
\texttt{min\_abs\_corr = 0}. Under the package defaults, an intercept was
included, residual variance was estimated, and credible sets targeted
95\% posterior coverage. Predictor columns were centered internally for
the intercept but were not scaled to unit variance. Setting the minimum
absolute correlation to zero imposed no positive within-set purity
threshold. Other fitting controls, including the iteration limit,
convergence tolerance, and initialization, used the defaults of the GTEx
run's \texttt{susieR} version [TO INSERT: version and default settings].
The same donors and processed phenotype were used for both models.
Uniform prior inclusion weights were used over the retained columns of
each design matrix. Consequently, the unweighted mixed analysis assigns
equal prior weight to SNP--coding pairs; total prior weight for a
biological SNP depends on how many of its encodings survive filtering.

We additionally fitted a weighted mixed-coding model to assess sensitivity
to the coding prior. The specified total prior masses were 0.80 for
additive, 0.15 for dominant, and 0.05 for recessive coding. If $m_c$ columns
of coding class $c$ remained, each received weight proportional to
$\pi_c/m_c$, with weights normalized over all retained predictors.
Thus, each represented coding class retained its specified total mass;
if a class was absent, its mass was redistributed proportionally among
the represented classes. These weights were supplied through
\texttt{prior\_weights}; all other fitting settings were unchanged.
The mixed analyses fit ordinary SuSiE on the expanded matrix. They impose
no additional constraint preventing different components from selecting
different encodings of the same biological SNP.

\subsection{Marginal association, permutation controls, and GTEx summaries}
For every retained additive SNP, we fitted the marginal regression of
$y$ on an intercept and that SNP's allele count, and recorded the
two-sided coefficient $P$ value. The smallest such value was the
gene--tissue marginal association statistic. The primary descriptive
comparison retained gene--tissue pairs with minimum marginal $P<10^{-8}$,
mean raw gene count at least 100, and available credible-set counts and
SNP-overlap summaries for both models. This selection used additive
marginal association, including when evaluating mixed-coding models.
Weighted-model comparisons additionally required an available weighted
fit. The descriptive scripts did not impose a separate convergence
filter on the GTEx fits.

For a negative control, the final processed phenotype was randomly
permuted across the matched donors. The random seed was reset to 1
within each gene--tissue analysis, and the same permutation was used
for the additive and unweighted mixed models. Each model was refitted
once using its original design matrix and fitting settings. The
implementation did not fit a weighted mixed model to the permuted
phenotype. We summarized the number of credible sets and the proportion
of analyses returning at least one credible set after permutation.
For the primary comparison, these quantities were evaluated in the
analysis set selected using the unpermuted data. They describe behavior
under this permutation control and do not estimate credible-set
coverage in the real GTEx data.

Credible-set members were mapped from predictor indices to biological
SNP identifiers, collapsing repeated encodings of the same SNP within
each set. We compared the number of reported credible sets, overlap
between the unions of credible-set SNPs, and the sets of lead biological
SNPs. For analyses with exactly one credible set from each model,
identical SNP sets were classified as full overlap, intersecting but
nonidentical SNP sets as partial overlap, and disjoint sets as no overlap.
For analyses with multiple credible sets, any overlap between the two
unions was distinguished from agreement of individual sets or lead
variants.

The lead predictor in a credible set was the member with the largest
single-effect posterior assignment probability in the component
generating that set. For older objects lacking a valid component index,
the highest predictor-level posterior inclusion probability within the
set was used instead; ties were resolved by the first maximizing member.
Mixed-model credible sets were summarized by the encoding of their
lead predictor, and gene--tissue pairs were classified by the combination
of lead encodings across their sets. These labels summarize the fitted
models and do not establish a biological mode of action. Lead-position
summaries used genomic SNP position minus TSS, without reversing the
sign for negative-strand genes. The plotted distributions were restricted
to $\pm 200$ kb and used 10-kb bins; the fitted cis window remained
$\pm 500$ kb.

\subsection{Simulation design and genotype sampling}

The completed benchmark was generated by \texttt{sim\_workhorse.R} and
the R jobs written by \texttt{write\_jobs.R}. We fixed the sample size
at $n=500$ and the fitted upper bound at $L_{\mathrm{fit}}=10$.
For every true causal count $K=1,\ldots,5$, we enumerated all
nonnegative integer triples $(K_A,K_R,K_D)$ summing to $K$, where zero
excludes that generating coding. There are 55 such allocations. Each
was crossed with PVE $h\in\{0.10,0.20,0.30,0.40\}$, yielding 220
conditions with 400 scheduled replications per condition. Single-coding
architectures permit $K=1,\ldots,5$, two-coding architectures
$K=2,\ldots,5$, and the three-coding architecture $K=3,\ldots,5$.
The true count $K$ is denoted by ``L'' in the PIP figure titles; it is
distinct from the fixed fitted bound of ten components.

For each replication, one pre-extracted GTEx PLINK genotype file was
sampled uniformly from the available \texttt{.raw} files. Thus, the
sampling frame was the available genotype-file collection rather than
a new draw from a genome-wide gene list. SNPs with any missing
genotype or no variation were removed. Across all donors in that file,
genotypes were oriented to the minor allele, SNPs were required to
have minor-allele frequency strictly greater than 0.05, and SNPs with
a Pearson Hardy--Weinberg chi-square $P$ value below $10^{-8}$ were
excluded. We then sampled 500 distinct donors without replacement,
retaining the full-donor allele orientation and local genotype
correlations. No additional MAF/HWE filtering or LD pruning was applied
to this donor subset.

For minor-allele dosage $G_{ij}\in\{0,1,2\}$, the candidate predictors
were $G_{ij}$ (additive), $\mathbf{1}\{G_{ij}\geq1\}$ (dominant), and
$\mathbf{1}\{G_{ij}=2\}$ (recessive). Each coding was filtered separately
to require positive sample standard deviation and a column sum of at
least five. This requires five allele copies for additive predictors,
five carriers for dominant predictors, or five minor-allele homozygotes
for recessive predictors; it does not require five donors in every
genotype class. Consequently, the retained coding blocks can have
different numbers of columns.

The causal-SNP pool was the intersection of the retained additive SNPs
and the retained SNPs of every non-additive coding requested by the
allocation. We sampled $K$ distinct biological SNPs uniformly without
replacement from this common pool and assigned the first $K_A$ additive,
the next $K_R$ recessive, and the final $K_D$ dominant effects. Each
causal SNP had exactly one generating coding, and there were no
interactions. In conditions containing recessive effects, this common
eligibility rule also restricts the additive and dominant causal SNPs
to those retaining a recessive predictor. An ineligible draw or a
zero-variance genetic signal produced a saved error without drawing a
replacement locus. No tissue-specific expression phenotype, covariate
adjustment, or inverse-normal transformation was used in the simulation.

\subsection{Effect sizes, LD and phenotype generation}

Let $W_{ik}$ be the generating encoding of causal SNP $k$ in donor $i$,
and let $\overline{W}_k$ and $s_k$ be its sample mean and standard
deviation. We standardized each causal predictor and independently
sampled signs $a_k\in\{-1,1\}$ with equal probabilities:

\begin{equation}
z_{ik}=\frac{W_{ik}-\overline{W}_k}{s_k},\qquad
 g_i^{(0)}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}a_k z_{ik}.
\end{equation}

For target PVE $h$, we applied one common rescaling to all effects:

\begin{equation}
b=\sqrt{\frac{h}{\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(g^{(0)})}},\qquad
 g_i=b g_i^{(0)},\qquad y_i=g_i+\epsilon_i,\qquad
 \epsilon_i\stackrel{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}N(0,1-h).
\end{equation}

Sample variance uses denominator $n-1$. This gives
$\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(g)=h$ while accounting for covariance among
causal predictors. Each individual contribution $b a_k z_k$ has the
same variance $b^2$; in LD this is not an allocation of $h/K$ to each
SNP, because the variance of the sum also includes covariance terms.
The target PVE is defined as sample genetic variance divided by sample
genetic variance plus the generating noise variance. Realized
$\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(g)/\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(y)$ can differ from
$h$ because the noise is an independent Gaussian draw. Errors were
neither orthogonalized against the genotypes nor rescaled after drawing.

\subsection{Model fitting and SNP-level evaluation}

We fitted \texttt{susieR::susie} to the retained additive matrix and to
the concatenated additive, recessive, and dominant matrices. Both fits
used the same outcome, $L_{\mathrm{fit}}=10$,
\texttt{standardize = TRUE}, \texttt{estimate\_prior\_method = "optim"},
\texttt{coverage = 0.95}, \texttt{min\_abs\_corr = 0.5}, and
\texttt{max\_iter = 1000}. The true causal count, generating codings,
and residual variance were not supplied. Other controls used the
installed package defaults. The benchmark compares ordinary additive
and unweighted mixed fits, not the weighted or EM-reweighted coding
models. Uniform prior inclusion weights are over retained predictor
columns; in the mixed fit, total prior weight per biological SNP
therefore depends on the number of encodings surviving filtering.
The standardization, prior-variance optimizer, and purity threshold
differ from the GTEx analysis settings described above.

For SuSiE-mix, coding-specific posterior assignment probabilities were
summed within each single-effect component before calculating a PIP
for biological SNP $j$:

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{PIP}_j=1-\prod_{\ell\in\mathcal{A}}
 \left(1-\sum_{c\in C_j}\alpha_{\ell jc}\right).
\end{equation}

Here $C_j$ is the set of retained encodings of SNP $j$. The fitted
prior variances were retained when calling \texttt{susie\_get\_pip},
so its usual exclusion of inactive components was preserved. Final
coding-specific PIPs were not summed. Mixed credible sets were mapped
to unique biological SNP indices within each set, without reconstructing
new credible sets from SNP-level PIPs. A reported set was considered
covered if it contained at least one generating causal SNP, irrespective
of the member's encoding. This evaluates SNP localization, not correct
identification of a generating coding or a one-to-one correspondence
between components and causal SNPs.

Empirical coverage was the total number of covered reported sets divided
by the total number of reported sets. Credible-set recovery was the
number of generating causal SNPs appearing in the union of reported
sets divided by the total number of generating causal SNPs; each SNP
was counted once within a dataset. Runs with no reported set contributed
zero recovery and no sets to the coverage or purity denominator. Purity
was the mean of the saved minimum absolute within-set correlations,
using the fitted predictor encodings for each method. Singleton purity
was one. We did not recompute mixed-set purity on additive genotypes.
Coverage and purity therefore weight reported sets, whereas recovery
weights causal SNPs.

\subsection{PIP curves, aggregation and computational record}

At each common PIP threshold $t$, a SNP was selected when its PIP was
at least $t$. We summed true positive (TP) and false positive (FP)
counts across the included datasets and calculated power as TP divided
by all causal SNPs, false positive rate as FP divided by all noncausal
SNPs, and pooled empirical FDR as FP divided by TP plus FP. The last
quantity is a pooled false discovery proportion, not the unweighted
mean of per-dataset false discovery proportions. The no-discovery
endpoint was defined as zero power and zero empirical FDR.

Counts were evaluated on a common dense grid with steps of 0.001
between 0.01 and 0.99, logarithmically spaced tails down to $10^{-12}$
and up to $1-10^{-12}$, and endpoints at zero, one, and infinity.
Tied PIPs entered together. Curves join threshold-specific points in
decreasing threshold order; empirical-FDR curves were not sorted by
FDR or replaced by an optimized envelope. The numerical comparisons
near 10\% empirical FDR use the grid point immediately preceding the
first exceedance of 0.10 in this order. Thresholds and achieved rates
are supplied with the summary tables. These truth-based operating
points are descriptive evaluations, not a proposed threshold-selection
rule for data with unknown causal variants.

For plots at fixed $K$, all compatible allocations within an architecture
were pooled. For ``All L pooled'' plots, TP and FP counts were also
summed across all available $K$ before rates were recalculated. This is
equivalent to stacking the SNP observations; it does not average
per-$K$ rates or give each $K$ equal weight. Larger loci contribute
more noncausal SNPs, and higher causal counts contribute more causal
SNPs. In mixed architectures, causal counts admitting more allocations
also contribute more datasets. Available-case weights reflect the
small number of failed draws.

Replication $r$ used seed $1{,}000{,}000+r$, for $r=1,\ldots,400$,
with the same seeds reused across allocations and PVE values. The
archive comprised 221 checkpoints: 220 production-condition files and
an older overlapping checkpoint. Nine duplicate condition--seed records
were removed. Of 88,000 scheduled condition--replication records,
12 had zero genetic variance and 87,988 were usable; no included fit
was flagged as nonconverged. Errors were omitted from both methods
rather than counted as no-discovery outcomes. Saved objects contain
settings, seeds, the genotype-file path, causal SNPs and codings,
standardized effects, convergence status, credible sets, and PIPs;
they do not retain the simulated outcome, sampled donor indices, or
full fitted models.

The figures included here display pooled point estimates without
Monte Carlo uncertainty intervals. The shared seeds define replication
blocks across conditions and must be retained together when assessing
uncertainty or comparing methods. The current figure set does not
include a credible-set-size comparison or PIP reliability diagram.
The analysis scripts, plotting code, checkpoint audit, configuration
counts, and numerical summaries document the reported results. The
cluster's R and package versions and the immutable genotype-file
inventory were not recorded in these compact objects and remain to be
added to the final reproducibility record.
